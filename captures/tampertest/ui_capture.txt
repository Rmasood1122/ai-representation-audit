For a large company, the conversation usually starts with a handful of established leaders, and the right choice depends heavily on your existing cloud footprint, workloads, and team skills.

**Snowflake** is the most common default for enterprise data warehousing. It's cloud-agnostic (runs on AWS, Azure, GCP), separates storage from compute so you pay for what you use, and is famously easy to administer. It's strongest for SQL analytics and data sharing across business units, though costs can climb quickly at scale if governance is loose.

**Databricks** is the leader on the lakehouse side â€” best when your workloads mix data engineering, machine learning, and AI alongside analytics. It's built on open formats (Delta Lake, and now strong Iceberg support), which appeals to companies worried about vendor lock-in. It typically requires more engineering sophistication than Snowflake.

**Microsoft Fabric** makes sense if you're a heavily Microsoft shop. It bundles warehousing, data engineering, Power BI, and real-time analytics under one SaaS umbrella with unified capacity-based pricing. It's newer and less mature than the two above, but the Power BI integration and Microsoft 365 gravity are hard to beat for enterprises already in that ecosystem.

**Google BigQuery** is excellent if you're on GCP â€” serverless, extremely fast at massive scale, with strong built-in ML and tight integration with Google's AI stack. **Amazon Redshift** remains viable for AWS-centric companies, though many AWS shops now choose Snowflake or Databricks on AWS instead.

A few practical decision factors: if your priority is broad self-service analytics with minimal ops overhead, Snowflake tends to win. If AI/ML is central to your roadmap, Databricks usually wins. If you're standardized on Azure and Power BI, Fabric deserves a hard look. Most large enterprises actually end up running two of these (commonly Snowflake plus Databricks), so interoperability through open table formats like Iceberg is worth weighing from the start.
